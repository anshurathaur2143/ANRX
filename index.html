How a BA works

From discovery to business acceptance.

A lifecycle overview. The detailed techniques belong in their dedicated topic pages, so this section stays focused on sequence and purpose.

01 / DISCOVER

Elicit

Understand goals, stakeholders, pain points, assumptions, and constraints.

02 / REFINE

Analyse

Resolve gaps, assess feasibility, model processes, and clarify business rules.

03 / DEFINE

Specify

Write requirements, stories, acceptance criteria, and traceability links.

04 / DELIVER

Collaborate

Support refinement, answer team questions, and assess change impact.

05 / VALIDATE

Verify

Support testing and UAT, resolve defects, and confirm business acceptance.

Answer structure

  • Start with the business objective, scope, known constraints, and decision-makers.
  • Select techniques based on context: interviews for individual detail, workshops for alignment, observation for actual workflows, and document review for existing rules.
  • Capture needs, assumptions, open questions, and conflicting viewpoints; validate the findings with stakeholders.
Example angle: explain one HRMS workflow, who you consulted, and how you confirmed the requirement before documenting it.

Elicitation discovers and draws out stakeholder needs. Analysis evaluates and refines what was discovered so it is consistent, feasible, prioritised, and testable.

  • Elicitation output: findings, needs, assumptions, questions, and stakeholder perspectives.
  • Analysis output: clarified requirements, business rules, models, gaps, dependencies, and decisions requiring approval.
Senior-level angle: give an example where analysis exposed an exception or conflict that was not obvious during the initial discussion.
  • Clarify the underlying goal and impact of each request rather than choosing a side immediately.
  • Compare business value, regulatory or operational constraints, risk, urgency, effort, and dependencies.
  • Facilitate a decision with the accountable owner, document the rationale, and update scope and traceability.
Avoid saying “I decide what gets built.” Explain how you make trade-offs visible and help the right decision-maker agree.
  • Describe observable behaviour and clear conditions, including valid, invalid, boundary, and exception scenarios where relevant.
  • Use Given / When / Then when it makes the expected outcome easier to understand.
  • Review criteria with stakeholders, developers, and QA; ensure each criterion can be verified.
Example: for leave requests, define eligibility, balance checks, approval routing, and the expected response when the balance is insufficient.
  • Confirm business scenarios, test data, roles, and acceptance criteria with users.
  • Coordinate execution, record evidence and defects, clarify expected behaviour, and support retesting.
  • Summarise coverage, open issues, risks, and deviations for the authorised business owner to make the sign-off decision.
Keep the distinction clear: QA may lead system testing; the BA helps ensure business needs are represented and validated.
  • Context: explain the workforce problem and the users involved, without exposing confidential client details.
  • Responsibility: name the modules and requirements you personally owned or supported.
  • Complexity: describe one rule, dependency, integration, or exception that needed clarification.
  • Delivery: explain how you documented, aligned stakeholders, supported development/QA, and validated the outcome.
Use a truthful, specific story. Do not claim ownership of modules or metrics you cannot substantiate.
  • Clarify the reason, expected outcome, urgency, and acceptance conditions.
  • Trace affected requirements, workflows, data, integrations, reports, test cases, and downstream modules.
  • Work with delivery leads to estimate effort and risks; present options and obtain approval before baselined scope changes.
  • Update documentation, backlog, traceability, and regression coverage after the decision.
  • Translate a business question into the data needed and identify relevant tables, keys, filters, and relationships.
  • Use SELECT and WHERE to inspect records; joins to connect entities; GROUP BY and aggregates to summarise results.
  • Validate counts and edge cases, reconcile against trusted outputs, and communicate limitations or data-quality issues.
Explain how the query supports a business decision or validation—not just the SQL syntax.
